## Reading the decision

The plan has three `### Task N:` headings, and the record's three cards are those ordinals in order:
Task 1 → **T-0648** (`asf/tick/steps.py`, `tests/test_tick.py`, `tests/test_clocks.py`), Task 2 →
**T-0649** (`asf/doctor.py`, `tests/test_doctor.py`), Task 3 → **T-0650** (the two guide pages and
`tests/test_readme.py`). The `writes:` sets of the record's three cards match the plan's three
headings one for one, so the mapping is not a guess. "Cut to Task 1" keeps the first and cuts the
other two.

`after:` moves with them. T-0650 carried `after: T-0648, T-0649`; it is dropped, so the edge goes
with the card. T-0648 and T-0649 were both `after: none` (the plan's PD9 measured them independent
in both directions), so dropping T-0649 strands no dependency and T-0648 keeps `after: none`. **No
surviving `after:` names a dropped card.**

**The reading rejected.** "Cut to Task 1" could be read as keeping Task 1 *and* Task 2 on the
grounds that the spec calls Task 2 the guard that makes Task 1 safe, and that a Feature shipping the
hazard without the guard is not what anyone wants. Against: the decision names a Task, not a
property, and Task 2 is a whole Story (S-37951) with its own footprint, its own acceptance fence
(§3.3) and its own five tests — it is the second of the plan's three Tasks by every reading of the
plan's own anatomy, and the plan's PD9 is an argument that it is *independent* of Task 1, not that
it is part of it. Keeping it would be the replan overruling the decision because the replan dislikes
the result. The decision is the scope. What this document owes the operator instead is the hazard,
stated once, precisely, where it cannot be missed — RD5.
